Why This Matters — The Practical ProblemWhen a client comes to Helio wanting to build 4 units on a serviced parcel that is zoned for 4 units, the answer is often 'not yet.' Two instruments from the parcel's past can sit on title and block construction — even though the municipality today wants exactly that density to happen. This playbook is your complete guide to identifying those instruments, understanding what they are, and removing them so the project can proceed.The permit chain: The Nova Scotia Building Code Regulations require that all applicable requirements — including any land use bylaw, subdivision bylaw, lot grading plan, or development agreement — have been complied with before a building permit can be issued. If a development agreement restricts use to single-family and you apply for a 4-unit permit, the development officer will refuse. The DA is the municipal gate.Covenants are different: HRM does not enforce private restrictive covenants when processing permit applications. If the proposed use meets the Land Use Bylaw, the permit will issue regardless of whether a covenant exists on title. However, the original covenant holder (often the original subdivider) can take the owner to NS Supreme Court to enforce it. The covenant discharge is about eliminating civil litigation risk — it is the private law gate.KEY DISTINCTION — Municipal vs. PrivateDevelopment Agreement (DA): A contract between the property owner and HRM. Enforced at the permit counter. Must be discharged through Community Council (§244) before a permit will issue for non-conforming uses.Restrictive Covenant: A contract between private parties (e.g., original developer and lot buyers). HRM will not refuse a permit because of it. The risk is civil litigation from the covenant holder. Discharged through the HRM CAO (§257A) or negotiated release.2. Understanding the Instruments2.1  Development Agreements (DAs)A development agreement is a registered contract between a property owner and HRM that governs how land may be developed. It is negotiated as part of a planning approval — for example, when the original developer of a subdivision wanted to build uses not permitted as-of-right under the then-applicable Land Use Bylaw. The DA was the municipality's tool for saying 'we'll allow this, but only under these conditions.'DAs are registered at the Registry of Deeds (Land Registration Office) as encumbrances on title. They bind all subsequent owners. The DA from 2004 that permitted a subdivision on a parent parcel is now binding on every lot that was created from that parent — even though the current owner has no connection to the original developer.What DAs commonly restrict for residential parcels: single-family dwelling use only; prohibition on subdivision; caps on unit count; specific dwelling footprint or height requirements; lot coverage maximums; tree preservation obligations; requirements to construct specific infrastructure before development.HOW A DA ENDS UP ON YOUR CLIENT'S LOTThe original developer (Ramar Developments, Twin Oaks Homes, etc.) applied to HRM to subdivide a large parcel into lots. As a condition of approval, HRM required them to sign a DA — typically restricting lots to single-family use, setting servicing standards, and requiring certain infrastructure. That DA was registered on the parent parcel and flowed through to each lot as it was conveyed. The current owner never agreed to it — they inherited it with the title.2.2  Restrictive CovenantsA restrictive covenant is an agreement between private parties — typically the developer and each lot purchaser — imposing obligations on how the land may be used. Unlike a DA, the counterparty is a private party (the developer or their successors), not the municipality.In standard subdivision practice, the original developer registers a covenant package at the time of selling lots. These covenants enforce the aesthetic and use character of the subdivision: single-family dwellings only, minimum square footage requirements, restrictions on outbuildings, fencing rules, architectural approval requirements, and tree removal restrictions. Their purpose was to protect the value of all lots in the subdivision — and to protect the developer's brand during active sales.InstrumentCounterpartyEnforcerBlocks Permit?Discharge PathDevelopment AgreementHRMDevelopment Officer at permit counterYes§244 — Community Council resolutionRestrictive CovenantOriginal developer or building schemeAny covenant beneficiary in NS Supreme CourtNot at HRM§257A — CAO administrative decisionEasement (utility/access)NSPI or adjacent ownerCivil courtNot at HRMNot in scope — requires consent of easement holder3. How to Read a Title and Identify What You Have3.1  Nova Scotia Property Online (NSPOL)Every title search in Nova Scotia starts at the NS Property Online system. Go to:NSPOL AccessURL: https://propertyonline.novascotia.caSearch by PID (Property Identification Number). Pull the 'Abstract of Title' — this is the chronological list of all instruments registered against the parcel.Document cost: $1.00–$2.50 per page. Budget $10–$30 for a full instrument pull on most residential lots.3.2  What to Look For in the AbstractThe abstract lists every registered instrument by type, instrument number, and registration date. You are looking for three categories:Document type 'DEVELOPMENT AGREEMENT' — This is a §244 DA. Note the instrument number (format: D_XXXXXXXX or similar), Book/Page reference, and registration date. Pull the full text. The key clauses to find: permitted use, unit count limit, expiry/commencement provisions, and any clause that states 'in accordance with the terms of this agreement' (this tells you whether discharge requires owner consent or is unilateral).Document type 'RESTRICTIVE COVENANT' or 'DEED RESTRICTION' — This is a §257A covenant. Note the instrument number and the name of the grantor. Pull the full text. The key clauses: any use restriction (single-family, residential only), any prohibition on subdivision or multi-unit development, and any clause requiring written approval of the original developer for changes.Document type 'EASEMENT' or 'RIGHT OF WAY' — These govern what happens on a strip of land (utility corridors, access routes). They are not in scope for discharge unless they conflict with your building envelope, in which case you need the easement holder's consent, not an HRM application.WHAT A DA ENTRY LOOKS LIKE IN THE ABSTRACTINSTRUMENT TYPE:   DEVELOPMENT AGREEMENTINSTRUMENT NO.:    D_84103291REGISTRATION DATE: 2007-06-14GRANTOR:           TWIN OAKS HOMES LTD.GRANTEE:           MUNICIPALITY OF HALIFAXBOOK/PAGE:         10447/391What this tells you: The DA was registered in 2007. Path A — Development Agreement Discharge (§244 HRM Charter)4.1  The Legal MechanismSection 244 of the HRM Charter states that Council may discharge a development agreement, in whole or in part, in accordance with the terms of the agreement or with the concurrence of the property owner. No public hearing is required. A Community Council may discharge by resolution.The 2023 amendments to the HRM Charter went further: they gave HRM the authority to unilaterally discharge existing development agreements that are more restrictive than current zoning with respect to height or density. This means that for urban/serviced parcels where the current zone allows more density than the DA, staff now have explicit legislative backing to recommend discharge even without a complex applicant justification.4.2  The Test for ApprovalStaff's recommendation — and Community Council's decision — must be 'reasonably consistent with the intent of the applicable Municipal Planning Strategy.' This is the central phrase. Your cover letter must make this argument. It has two parts:The DA is spent, moot, or its purpose is no longer applicable. The development it permitted has been completed; or the original developer no longer controls the land and the intentions behind the DA no longer apply; or the DA restricts to single-family on a parcel that is now designated for multi-unit residential in the current MPS.Discharging the DA is consistent with current planning policy. The current zone permits the proposed use as-of-right or by development agreement. Provincial housing legislation prioritizes increasing supply. The MPS identifies the area as appropriate for infill residential intensification.WHAT 'REASONABLY CONSISTENT WITH THE MPS' MEANS IN PRACTICEYou do not need to prove that discharge is the only correct outcome — you need to show it is a reasonable planning decision. HRM staff will evaluate whether the underlying LUB zone already governs the parcel adequately without the DA. If the zone allows multi-unit development and the DA restricts it, the argument writes itself.Key phrase to use in your cover letter: 'The proposed discharge is consistent with the intent of the [applicable] Municipal Planning Strategy, which designates the subject lands for residential infill and supports increasing housing density in established neighbourhoods.'4.3  Documents to GatherDocumentWhere to Get ItNotesFull text of the DA instrumentNSPOL — pull by Book/Page referenceCritical — you must cite specific clauses in your cover letter. Path B — Restrictive Covenant Discharge (§257A HRM Charter)5.1  The Legal MechanismSection 257A of the HRM Charter — added through amendments in 2023 — allows the Chief Administrative Officer (CAO) of HRM to discharge or modify private restrictive covenants when they are more restrictive than the current zoning with respect to height or density. This is a purely administrative decision; it does not go to Council, and no public hearing is required.Critically: even if the CAO declines to discharge the covenant, HRM could still issue a building permit for a proposed use that meets the Land Use Bylaw. The municipality does not enforce private covenants when processing permit applications. The §257A discharge is about eliminating the civil enforcement risk, not unblocking the permit counter.5.2  The Test: 'More Restrictive Than Zoning'The statutory test is narrow and specific: the covenant must be more restrictive than the current zoning with respect to height or density. For typical infill scenarios, this is easy to satisfy:Density gap: The covenant says single-family dwelling only. The current LUB zone permits 4-unit or multi-unit residential. The zone allows more units than the covenant — the covenant is more restrictive on density. §257A applies.Height gap: The covenant caps building height at 1.5 storeys or 25 feet. The current zone permits up to 4 storeys. The zone allows more height than the covenant. §257A applies.No gap: The covenant says single-family only AND the current zone only permits single-family. The covenant and zoning are aligned — the 'more restrictive than zoning' argument does not automatically apply. This is the harder case (e.g., R-1B parcels). A legal assessment of whether §257A can still be argued on a future rezoning basis is worth the hour of a lawyer's time before filing.5.3  Documents to GatherDocumentWhere to Get ItNotesFull text of the covenant instrumentNSPOL — pull by instrument numberYou must attach this as an exhibit to your rationale letter. Annotated Case StudiesThe following cases are drawn from actual HRM PLANAPP filings. Each is annotated to show what argument was made and why it worked.Case Study 1: DA Discharge — 1749–1759 Vernon Street, HalifaxCase reference: Former HRM Case #17371, discharged 2019 (HWCC staff report May 2019)Instrument: Development Agreement, registered Feb 13, 2013 as Document 102476869Original purpose of the DA: Permitted an increase in the volume of a non-conforming 6-unit apartment house on Halifax Peninsula.WHAT HAPPENEDThe original owner obtained a DA in 2012 to expand the existing 6-unit apartment building. They received two timeline extensions (2015 and 2017) due to personal circumstances. In December 2018, ERZ Properties Limited purchased the property. The new owner had no intention of proceeding with the expansion and applied to discharge the DA.The argument: The DA was moot — the development it was designed to permit was never going to happen. The new owner's plans did not require the DA's permissions. Discharging it would simply return the parcel to being governed by the standard LUB zone. Staff confirmed this was 'reasonably consistent with the intent of the Halifax MPS.'The outcome: No public objections. No public hearing. Community Council approved by resolution. The parcel was returned to standard LUB zone governance. Process took approximately 3–4 months.Lesson: A DA does not need to be harmful or burdensome to discharge — it just needs to be no longer applicable. A change of ownership and change of development intent is sufficient grounds when the standard zone is adequate to govern the property.Case Study 2: Covenant Discharge (§257A) — PLANAPP 2025-01950 & 2025-01952Properties: Two properties in a zone now designated for 9-storey mixed-use developmentCovenant restriction: 'No building shall be erected nearer than twenty feet from any city street' — plus single-family use restrictionCurrent zone: CEN-2 (Centre 2) — allows up to 9 storeys, residential and commercial usesWHAT HAPPENEDBoth properties were single-family homes subject to a covenant from the era when the neighbourhood was developed as a residential street. The current zone designation reflects decades of planning evolution — the area is now an urban intensification zone.The argument: The covenant restricted use and height/setback in ways that were entirely inconsistent with the 9-storey mixed-use zone the properties now sit in. The covenant was 'more restrictive than current zoning with respect to height and density' — the exact §257A test. Applicants submitted a rationale letter quoting the restrictive clause, cited the CEN-2 zone standards, and showed the gap.Two applications, not one: These were filed as two separate PLANAPPs (01950 and 01952) because the covenant ran on two separate PIDs. If your client has multiple lots subject to the same covenant, file separate applications per PID.The outcome: Public engagement closed October 7, 2025. Staff preparing report to the CAO. Applications progressing to decision.Lesson: The §257A argument is strongest when the zoning has evolved dramatically since the covenant was written — e.g., a 1980s single-family covenant on a parcel now in a high-density urban zone. The wider the gap between what the covenant restricts and what the zone permits, the easier the rationale letter is to write.Case Study 3: Partial Covenant Discharge — PLANAPP 2025-01839, 43 Braeloch Court, Lake LoonApplication: Discharge of clauses 1, 2, and 16 only — not the entire covenantFiled by: Property owner directly (no consulting firm)WHAT THIS ILLUSTRATESYou do not have to discharge an entire covenant. If only specific clauses block your project, you can target those clauses in your application. This approach reduces the scope of the application and can make it easier to argue because you are not asking to remove all covenant restrictions — just the ones that conflict with the proposed use.Clause-by-clause strategy: Pull the full covenant instrument. Map each clause against your proposed project. Clauses that actively block the project (single-family restriction, subdivision prohibition, approval requirement from original developer) are your targets. Clauses that are neutral or already unenforceable (e.g., architectural approval clauses where the original approver no longer exists) can be left.Also notable: This application was filed by the property owner directly — not by a consulting firm. It is evidence that the process is accessible to a non-professional applicant who knows what they are doing.Lesson: Think surgically. A targeted partial discharge is often easier to argue and less likely to attract opposition than a full discharge of all covenant terms.Case Study 4: Pure DA Discharge — 2900 Agricola Street, Halifax (Bell Media)Applicant: Bell Media Inc.DA instrument: Former HRM Case — permitted construction of a radio stationContext: Development is complete; applicant proposes a change of use for the existing buildingWHAT HAPPENEDBell Media's DA permitted the construction of a radio station. The station was built. Bell Media then wanted to change the use of the building. The DA, written to permit the radio station, was now the wrong instrument — the change of use required a clean title governed only by the underlying LUB zone.The argument: The development the DA was written for is complete. The DA is fully performed. The proposed discharge returns the parcel to governance under the CEN-2 zone standards — which permit the proposed change of use as-of-right.The outcome: Approved by Community Council by resolution. No public hearing. Staff confirmed 'reasonably consistent with the MPS.'Lesson: When a DA has been fully performed (the development it permitted has been built), the argument for discharge is at its simplest. State that the purpose of the DA has been fulfilled and that the underlying zone is now sufficient to govern the property.7.
